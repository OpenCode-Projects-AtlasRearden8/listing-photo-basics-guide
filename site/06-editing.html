Phone Editing Without Fakery

Editing listing photos is like ironing a shirt before you sell it: wrinkles gone, fabric untouched. The moment you change what the shirt is, you’ve crossed into fakery — and marketplaces treat fakery as a policy problem, not a style choice.

The honest line, stated once

Editing may fix the camera’s lies. It may not change the item’s facts.

  • Lighten a dark shot because the phone underexposed it: fine.
  • Brighten a shadow so you can see the actual color: fine (as long as the color you let buyers see is the real color).
  • Erase a scratch, crop out a crack, airbrush a stain: never. That’s the flaw you were legally obliged to show (see Honest condition shots).

A quick self-test: could a buyer holding the item compare it to your photo and say “yep, that’s it”? If yes, the edit is safe.

The five-minute edit that makes most listings look professional

In order, on almost any phone editor:

  1. Crop to the item. Nothing distracting at the edges, item nicely filling the frame.
  2. Straighten — a tilted horizon is the #1 “amateur” tell.
  3. Exposure up a touch (or down a touch for dark glossy items) so the image is faithfully readable.
  4. White balance / warmth until whites look white, not blue from window light or orange from indoors. Wrong warm cast makes cream look yellow — a classic “item not as described” trap.
  5. Sharpen very slightly if the photo came out soft; stop before halos appear.

That’s it. No ten-slider chains, no presets, two minutes of honest upkeep.

Edits to avoid — and why

Edit Why it goes wrong
Beauty/brightening filters Shift colors; buyers choose by color and disputes follow
Heavy saturation Makes bi-colored items look unrecognizably vivid
Exact-color shift by hand Dress looked navy; arrives black; returns result
Airbrushing blemishes Hides flaws you must disclose; marketplace policy issue
Removing backgrounds and adding fake props Fine for Amazon’s white-background MAIN images (see next page); bad elsewhere, distorts item context
Framing “collages” with text on photos Often disallowed (eBay explicitly); unreadable small anyway

Phone features worth saying yes to

  • Auto-crop/straighten in the phone’s photo app: fine, gives instant improvement.
  • “Enhance”/”Auto” in moderation — it’s just brightness/contrast adjustments done sensibly, and if it doesn’t alter colors or hide evidence, it saves time. Check after.
  • Lens-based perspective correction when it’s pure straightening (like straightening a building). Only use it if it doesn’t stretch the item’s proportions, which quietly ruins size perception.
  • The grid overlay — a photography setting, not an edit: turn it on. It centers the item and keeps lines level, which means less editing later.

Files: what to save

  • Keep the originals in a “RAW-ish” folder per item — before you crop or edit anything. If a dispute arrives weeks later, the untouched originals are your receipt that disclosure photos existed.
  • Export in JPEG at 1600–2000 px on the long side for eBay/Etsy (per the size habits page); Amazon likes 1600–3000 px for good zoom.
  • Don’t inflate resolution. Upscaling adds fake sharpness the buyer can see. Shoot bigger instead.
  • Rename files sanely: item-sku-03-flaw-closeup.jpg. Sorting by name beats sorting by phone-internals timestamps three months from now.

One habit that pays forever

Do the edits in the same order, in the same editor, at the same desk. When editing becomes muscle memory you’ll finish ten items in the time it takes to fumble with a single fancy filter — and every photo will look like it came from the same house, which is the cheapest brand-building there is.
