Backgrounds and Scale Props

Two problems every beginner listing has: busy backgrounds that fight the item, and no sense of size because the item floats alone in the void.

Backgrounds: three levels, all free-or-cheap

Level 1 — clean wall. A bare wall, shot from a few feet back, is fine for many items and better than any cluttered scene. Wipe it down first.

Level 2 — poster board or a hung sheet. When the wall itself is dark, textured, or busy:

  • White board / white sheet: the classic, works for most items, but grabs glare — diffuse first.
  • Kraft board (warm brown-gray): reduces glare, makes white items pop, feels “handmade.”
  • Mid-gray or colored board: necessary when the item is white or very light, or when glassware needs something dark behind it (see Light without a rig).

Level 3 — the “life” background. For Etsy-style shops especially, a styled background — the cutting board under the knife, the yarn behind the needles — helps brand a shop. Two rules: it must be tidier than real life, and the item must stay the brightest, sharpest thing in frame.

The seam trick (free)

A true studio look at $5: tape a sheet of poster board to the wall, let it curve onto the table, with a gentle bend — you get a wall and a floor with no corner line between them. The curve means no shadow line cutting through your frame.

Hanging and steadying

  • Painter’s tape holds board without marking walls.
  • Spring clips from a craft store keep a hung backdrop tight and wrinkle-free.
  • Wrinkles: fabric backdrops should be stretched tight and flat — a loose, in-focus wrinkle field mid-frame looks accidental. Iron it, stretch it, or blow-dry it gently.
  • Keep backdrop one plain color across the whole frame; gradients from your bounce light are fine, patterns are not.

Scale props: prove the size

Size is the most-asked question in resales. Bake the answer into the photos:

  • A standard ruler (both inches and centimeters if you sell internationally).
  • A coin — the universal close-up reference.
  • A hand — for reasonably-sized items, a hand in frame is instantly readable.
  • A common object with known size: a soda can, a shoebox, a playing card, a folded dollar bill.
  • Tape measure showing the measurement itself. For vintage clothing especially, lay the garment flat and photograph the tape laid across the chest, inseam, sleeve, and length.

Rules for scale shots:

  1. Keep the reference in the same plane as the item (flat on the table, not waving toward the camera), so it isn’t distorted by the phone’s wide-angle lens.
  2. Make the reference neutral and tidy — a crumpled receipt is honest but ugly; a small chalkboard tag with a number is honest and clean (write it in marker, legibly, only a real measurement).
  3. One reference per photo. A ruler and a coin and your hand in one frame is clutter.
  4. Keep scale props out of the main photo. The first image should be the cleanest, most flattering shot; scale lives in shots 3–5.

What never belongs in a listing photo

  • Your address, labels, or shipping slip in the background — that’s personal data walking around the internet.
  • Business cards or watermarks — eBay’s picture policy specifically disallows logos and watermarks, and they look amateur elsewhere.
  • Other items for other listings in the same frame — buyers get confused, and marketplaces suppress accidental bundle-looking listings.
  • Pets and kids parts — cute on social media, distracting (and allergy-triggering) in a purchase decision.
