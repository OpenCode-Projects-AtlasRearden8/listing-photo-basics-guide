Before/After Cleaning Shots

Selling used items well means telling the truth twice: here’s what it looked like, and here’s what I did about it. A quick before/after pair does both at once, and it makes buyers trust every other photo in the listing.

When to shoot a “before”

Reach for the “before” photo whenever your prep is more than a wipe:

Situation Shoot “before”? Why
Surface dust, quick wipe-down No Dust is expected on used items; no claim to prove
Sticky residue, grime, finger marks on visible areas Yes Shows the residue existed and came off
Polishing dull or tarnished metal Yes Proves the shine in your “after” is real
Deep-cleaning a garment before listing Yes Shows brightening; pairs well with cleaning notes
Limescale or hard-water marks on glass/ceramics Yes Without proof, buyers worry it’s etching, which won’t come off
Straightening the box or packaging No No accuracy claim is being made

Rule of thumb: if your claim is “I cleaned or improved it,” shoot the before. If your claim is just “it’s in this condition,” your listing photos are the after and no pair is needed.

How to shoot the pair fairly

Fairness is the whole game — a manipulative before/after reads instantly as manipulation:

  • Same camera, same corner, same light. Same table, same distance, same backdrop, same exposure tap. Do them back-to-back, minutes apart.
  • Same angle, same framing. The “after” should look exactly like the “before” with one variable changed: how clean the item is.
  • Frame the specific spot. A close-up of the exact area you cleaned beats a wide re-shot.
  • Note the tools in the description: “wiped with vinegar rinse, dried flat” reads like honest care in half a sentence.
  • No staged dirt. Never exaggerate the “before” — shooting it badly on purpose or adding grime poisons the trust you’re trying to build. Savvy buyers can smell it.

Where the pair goes in the listing

  • Never in slot 1. The main photo does the click-catching; before/after pairs tell the care story behind it.
  • Put the pair near the front (slots 2–3) for heavily cleaned or restored items, and later (slots 6–8) when it’s just routine prep.
  • Caption the pair in the description, not on the photos. Marketplaces often disallow text overlays on photos (eBay’s picture policy is explicit), and text is unreadable at thumbnail size anyway.

Items where cleaning notes matter most

  • Fabric and clothing: wash or steam when safe, air out overnight, and describe what you did (“laundered, line-dried”). Photos can’t carry scent claims — the description must. Insist on the facts only: nothing you can’t back up, and no way of speaking about “smoke-free home” claims that you can’t honestly stand behind.
  • Electronics: wipe the screen and port covers, but photograph them honestly if they’re not perfect (see Honest condition shots); put “tested working” in the description words.
  • Vintage hard goods with old residue: clean once, photograph honestly wherever residue remains, and disclose it plainly.

One habit that pays forever

Clean the item fully — before the first photo, not after. It’s easier to sell “surprisingly spotless” than “clean, but there’s an unexplained spot.” If it can’t be fully cleaned, that’s a condition fact, not an embarrassment: shoot that spot straight-on, with scale so its size is clear (see Backgrounds and scale).
